\chapter{MLS Level~3 datasets}
\label{chap:Level3}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:L3Intro}

The other chapters of this guide describe MLS ``Level 2'' data --
geophysical products along the instrument observing track. This
chapter describes MLS ``Level 3'' data -- geophysical products binned
onto regular grids. There is a wide array of techniques for
transforming along-track observations into regular grids, including
``kriging'' approaches {[}e.g., \emph{Cressie}, 2015{]},
Fourier-series-based approaches {[}\emph{Salby}, 1982{]}, approaches
using various windowing functions such as Hamming windows, and simple
binning approaches. All methods have strengths and weaknesses, and the
choice of which one to use is best made by the individual data user,
based on the nature of the specific scientific question to be
tackled. For this reason, until 2020, the MLS team had not produced
any ``official'' Level 3 data products.

However, in order to facilitate certain studies by MLS data users, the
MLS team is now producing and distributing a select set of Level 3
products derived using a simple ``binning'' approach (i.e., simply
reporting the mean measured value within a given spatial/temporal
``box''). As part of the generation of these data products, all the
data screening rules described in chapters 1 and 3 are applied (along
with the ClO bias correction, see section~\ref{sec:StandardClO}),
providing a significant additional simplification to data users.
Level~3 data are supplied for most MLS standard products.  BrO and
HO\cs2 are excluded, as they have have a different Level 3 product
available (see sections~\ref{sec:StandardBrO}
and~\ref{sec:StandardHO2}).  We also exclude CH\cs3CN and CH\cs3OH in
light of the narrow range over which these products are recommended
for scientific use.

Three sets of Level~3 products are provided:
\begin{description}
\item[Daily Binned Files:] These are stored as one product per day of
  MLS data per file. The daily binned files contain the following datasets:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Geodetic latitude and longitude maps on a pressure vertical
    coordinate (i.e., ``3D'' fields).
  \item Geodetic latitude and longitude maps on a
    potential-temperature vertical coordinate (i.e., ``3D'' fields).
  \item Geodetic latitude zonal means on a pressure vertical
    coordinate (``2D'').
  \item Geodetic latitude zonal means on a potential-temperature
    vertical coordinate (``2D'').
  \item Equivalent latitude\footnote{Equivalent latitude is the
      geographical latitude that encloses the same area as an isoline
      of potential vorticity, giving a polar vortex-centered latitude
      \citep{ButchartandRemsberg:1986}.} ``zonal means'' on a
    potential-temperature vertical coordinate (``2D'').
  \item Vortex averages on a potential-temperature vertical
    coordinate (``1D'').
  \end{itemize}
\item[Monthly Binned Files:] These are stored as one product per year
  of MLS data per file. The monthly binned files contain the same
  datasets as the daily binned files, except with twelve monthly
  entries in each dataset instead of one daily one.
\item[Zonal Mean Binned Files:] These files contain ``roll-ups'' of
  all the datasets in the daily binned files, excluding the ``3D''
  maps. These are provided with fields having 365 or 366 entries per
  dataset in each file.
\end{description}
For species with non-negligible diurnal variations over all or part of
their vertical range (ClO, OH, HOCl, and O\cs3), the datasets
described above are further subdivided into five different datasets
containing:
\begin{itemize}
\item All valid profiles (as for all the non-diurnal products).
\item All profiles measured on the ascending side of the orbit (mainly
  daytime observations, except in polar regions).
\item All profiles measured on the descending side of the orbit (mainly
  nighttime observations, except in polar regions).
\item All observations made with solar zenith angle less than 90\textdegree{} (i.e.,
  daytime observations).
\item All observations made with solar zenith angle greater than 110\textdegree{} (i.e.,
  nighttime observations).
\end{itemize}
MERRA-2 \citep{GelaroEtal:2017} reanalysis temperature fields
(interpolated to each MLS observation point) are used to convert from
pressure to potential temperature surfaces. Additionally MERRA-2
potential vorticity fields are used to construct equivalent latitude
and vortex average information. The interpolated MERRA-2 fields used
are taken from the MLS ``Derived Meteorological Products''
\citep{ManneyEtal:2007, ManneyEtal:2011:Jets}. Note that, for
quantities on potential temperature surfaces, the MLS Level 3 profiles
on pressure levels are first interpolated to the fixed potential
temperature surfaces (from $\log[p]$ to $\log[\theta]$) before being
binned (as opposed to directly binning Level~3 data points in
potential temperature windows, which has the potential to introduce
undesirable empty bins in the vertical). These interpolated values
(and the interpolated \verb+L2GPPrecision+ field)\mycomment{Question
  to Ryan, is this really relevant?  Does it somehow come into the

  but 0.46\,hPa's is negative, then data at any potential temperature
  surfaces that correspond to a pressure less than 0.1\,hPa will be

The vortex averages are defined using an altitude-dependent value of
scaled potential vorticity (sPV) to demark the vortex edge.  The
values at each level are determined separately for each hemisphere,
using the prodedure described by \citet{LawrenceEtal:2018} wherein a
constant sPV for each hemisphere and season is derived based on the
climatological sPV at the location of the maximum high-latitude sPV
gradient.  While there are numerous methods of estimating the location
of the vortex edge, this simple method with a value that is constant
in time has been shown to avoid some common pitfalls such as large
spurious day-to-day changes in vortex area and inappropriate values at
the beginning and end of the cold season \citep[see][for a discussion
of pros and cons of various vortex edge identification
methods]{LawrenceandManney:2018}.  To estimate uncertainties in vortex
edge definition, and to provide results for studies where more or less
conservative estimates of the vortex edge are desirable, averages are
also provided for ``outer'' and ``inner'' vortex edge values that are
0.2$\times 10^{-4} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ less and more, respectively, than the
determined value for the vortex edge ``center'' \citep[this also
follows][]{LawrenceEtal:2018}.

\section{Level~3 data files}
\label{sec:L3files}

Two different temporal resolutions of Level 3 data are
provided---daily (\texttt{L3DB}) and monthly (\texttt{L3MB}). The
\texttt{L3DB} files are one file per day per Level 3 data product
(with one time entry in each dataset in the file), and the
\texttt{L3MB} files are one file per year per Level~3 data product
(with twelve time entries in each dataset in the file). Both of these
files contain latitude/longitude grids, zonal means, and polar vortex
averages on various vertical coordinates, as described in
section~\ref{sec:L3Intro}, above. To aid in the analysis of long-term
datasets, the additional \texttt{L3DZ} files are a yearly rollup of
all of the \texttt{L3DB} datasets except for the latitude/longitude
grids, which are not included for file size considerations. The
datasets in \texttt{L3DZ} files contain 365 or 366 time entries as
appropriate. The filenames for these products using the following
conventions, which differ mainly in the date format:
\newcommand{\fileexample}[2][]{{\texttt{#2}} #1}
\begin{quote}\small
\fileexample{MLS-Aura\_L3DB-<product>\_v04-20-c01\_<yyyy>d<ddd>.nc}\\
\fileexample{MLS-Aura\_L3MB-<product>\_v04-20-c01\_<yyyy>.nc}\\
\fileexample{MLS-Aura\_L3DZ-<product>\_v04-20-c01\_<yyyy>.nc}\\
\fileexample[(Mid-year forward processing
only)]{MLS-Aura\_L3MB-<product>\_v04-20-c01\_<yyyy>m<mm>.nc}\\
\fileexample[(Mid-year forward processing only)]{MLS-Aura\_L3DZ-<product>\_v04-20-c01\_<yyyy>m<mm>.nc}
\end{quote}
Where \texttt{<product>} is the MLS standard product: \texttt{CO},
\texttt{O3}, \texttt{Temperature}, etc. The date(s) for the
observations are given as a four-digit year \texttt{<yyyy>} with an
optional three-digit day of year \texttt{<ddd>} (\texttt{001} = 1
January) or optional two-digit month \texttt{<mm>}. Files using the
two-digit month will only be available during their current calendar
year, as ``forward processing'' data are gathered, and will contain
all data in the year up to and including the quoted month, with the
remaining date entries present but consisting of values marked ``bad''
(i.e., with the netCDF \verb+_FillValue+).  Once all months are
available, the complete dataset will be stored in the
``\texttt{<yyyy>}'' files.

Within each file, the datasets are stored in netCDF-4 groups. The
\texttt{L3DB} and \texttt{L3MB} files contain six types of datasets:
latitude/longitude grids on both pressure and potential temperature
vertical coordinates,
zonal means on both pressure and potential temperature vertical
coordinates, means within equivalent latitude contours on potential
temperature surfaces, and polar vortex averages as a function of
potential temperature. As noted above, the \texttt{L3DZ} files omit
the latitude/longitude maps.

These datasets are named as follows (where \texttt{<product>} is the
same as listed in the filename):

  <product> PressureGrid: & 4\textdegree$\times$5\textdegree{} geodetic
  longitude/latitude grid on pressure surfaces (\texttt{L3DB} and
  \texttt{L3MB} only)\tabularnewline
  
  <product> ThetaGrid: & 4$\textdegree\times$5\textdegree{} geodetic
  longitude/latitude grid on potential temperature surfaces
  (\texttt{L3DB} and \texttt{L3MB} only)\tabularnewline
  
  <product> PressureZM: & 4\textdegree{} geodetic latitude zonal mean
  on pressure surfaces\tabularnewline

  <product> ThetaZM: & 4\textdegree{} geodetic latitude zonal mean on
  potential temperature surfaces\tabularnewline

  <product> EqLZM: & 4\textdegree{} equivalent latitude zonal mean on
  potential temperature surfaces\tabularnewline

  <product> VortexAvg: & Polar vortex average values for each
  hemisphere on potential temperature surfaces.  The values are
  calculated in accordance with the ``outer'', ``center'', and
  ``inner'' vortex edge criteria documented in
  \citet{LawrenceEtal:2018}.\tabularnewline
\end{tabular}

\vspace{\baselineskip}\noindent Diurnal product files have all of
these groups repeated for day, night, ascending profiles only, and
descending profiles only (with \texttt{Day}, \texttt{Night},

Each group contains the following data fields:

  lon\_bnds: & The longitude at the boundaries of the grid cell

  lat: & The latitude at center of the grid cell\tabularnewline

  lat\_bnds: & The latitude at the boundaries of the grid
  cell\tabularnewline

  time: & The date of the grid cell (days since
  1950-01-01)\tabularnewline

  time\_bnds: & The boundaries of the time period (days since
  1950-01-01)\tabularnewline

  lev: & The vertical coordinate (i.e. pressure/potential temperature

  value: & The average value for each bin\tabularnewline

  nvalues: & The number of valid data points found in each

  $\sqrt{\texttt{nvalues}}$)\tabularnewline

  std\_dev: & The standard deviation of the data in each

\end{tabular}

\section{Guidance for users of Level~3 data}

As discussed above, these Level~3 products are generated using only
the Level~2 data points that meet all the screening criteria described
in the earlier chapters of this document, providing a significant
simplification for users. In cases where there are no good Level~2
data available to populate a Level~3 data point, the \texttt{nvalues}
field is set to zero and the other data fields are set to the
\texttt{\_FillValue} (bad data flag) for the given netCDF variables.

When considering Level~3 data, as with every geophysical measurement,
attention should be paid to the associated uncertainties.  The
averaging inherent in generating Level~3 data will act to reduce
``precision''-related errors, those due to random noise in the MLS
radiance measurements, by $1/\sqrt{n}$, where $n$ is the number of
Level~2 data points in the average (which is given by the
\texttt{nvalues} field).  We have chosen not to divide by $\sqrt{n}$
in the files in order to make it easier for users who wish to perform
further averaging.  For example, when creating weekly averages from
the daily L3 data, the final precision can be computed as the
root-mean-square of the daily precisions divided by the square root of
the total of the daily \verb+nvalues+ entries.

In contrast, the ``accuracy''-related errors, those due to biases in
the MLS measurement system, will in most cases be propagated through
the averaging process unchanged, and should be assumed to apply to the
Level~3 values in the same manner as they do to the values in the
underlying Level~2 datasets.
