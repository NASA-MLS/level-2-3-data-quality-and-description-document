\subsection{Introduction}

The standard \ch{SO2} product is taken from the 240\nobreakdash-GHz retrieval (\texttt{CorePlusR3}). MLS can only measure \ch{SO2} when its concentrations are significantly enhanced above the nominal background, such as those from volcanic injections. Validation of \vii \ch{SO2} was reported by \citet{PumphreyEtal:2015}.

\subsection{Changes from \prevv}

Changes in the 240\nobreakdash-GHz MLS products from \prevv to \thisv are minor. The standard \ch{SO2} product is retrieved from the \texttt{CorePlusR3} phase that simultaneously retrieves tangent pressure, temperature, and its targeted species from 118- and 240\nobreakdash-GHz signals. Changes made to improve the tangent pressure and temperature will have some impact on the \ch{SO2} retrieval. Similarly, the new approach to cloud flagging discussed in Section~\ref{sec:V6CloudWork} should be noted.

Figure~\ref{fig:avgmlsso2prflcomp} compares \ch{SO2} in MLS \vii, \viii, \viv, \prevv, and \thisv. The atmosphere typically has \mytexttilde0.1\,ppbv \ch{SO2}, which is far smaller than the MLS \ch{SO2} accuracy estimate. All versions (erroneously) report typical \ch{SO2} abundances of the order of a few ppbv, due to systematic errors in the MLS measurement system. Changes from \vii through to \thisv largely result from modifications in the handling of background radiance signals, which evolved from being modeled as an absorption continuum (\vii) to a continuum modeled as water vapor absorption retrieved as relative humidity (\viii) to an MLS limb-pointing-based absorption continuum with a frequency squared spectral dependence (\viv through \thisv). Versions~\viv through \thisv also use improved cloud detection and radiance screening.

\subsection{Resolution}
\onestandardavk{SO2}{\ch{SO2}}%

Based on Figure~\ref{fig:AvkSO2}, the vertical resolution for \ch{SO2} is \mytexttilde3\,km, and the horizontal resolution is 170\,km. The horizontal resolution perpendicular to the orbit track is 7\,km, the full width at half maximum of the MLS antenna, for all pressures.

\subsection{Precision}

The estimated precision for \ch{SO2} is \mytexttilde4\,ppbv for all heights between 215 and 10\,hPa. The precisions are set to negative values (or zero in some cases) in situations when the retrieved precision is larger than 50\% of the a~priori precision --- an indication that the data are biased toward the a~priori value. The changes between \prevv and \thisv are minor and result in only small differences in the estimated precision. An example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:so2prec} for 100\,hPa on 14~February 2009, a day when no eruptions were detected by MLS\@. \ch{SO2} precision estimates from \prevv and \thisv show much less variability and less latitude dependence than was present in \viv.  Similar behavior is seen on other dates and at other stratospheric levels, as well as in other species retrieved from the 240\nobreakdash-GHz radiances, such as \ch{HNO3}. This change in behavior occurred as a result of removing some approximations in the forward model in \prevv and \thisv.

\subsection{Accuracy}

Based on comparisons with coincident OMI total column \ch{SO2} observations, the MLS \ch{SO2} retrievals at 215 and 147\,hPa occasionally exhibit spurious spikes and enhancements that are not reflected in the OMI measurements.  Accordingly, although the formal MLS systematic error assessment approach described by \citet{ReadEtal:2007} indicates smaller uncertainty, the MLS team recommends that the accuracy of the MLS \ch{SO2} at these pressure levels be considered to be 20 and 10\,ppbv at 215 and 147\,hPa, respectively.  Accuracies at other pressure levels are estimated using the approach taken by \citet{ReadEtal:2007}.

\subsection{Data screening}
\label{sec:SO2Screening}
\begin{description}
  \item[Pressure range:] \screeningrule{215--10.0\,hPa} \standardrangestatement
  \item[Estimated Precision:] \screeningrule{In general, as for other MLS species, retrieved \ch{SO2} values for which the associated precision is negative should not be used.  However, \ch{SO2} data showing strong ($>$20\,ppbv) enhancements that have negative precision may still be used.}

  Although it is generally recommended to not use values where precision is flagged negative, \ch{SO2} is an exception, and it is appropriate to use values with negatively flagged precision (provided that the entire profile is not so flagged otherwise passes the \texttt{Status}, \texttt{Quality}, and \texttt{Convergence} criteria). High retrieved values of \ch{SO2} at larger pressures (e.g., 215 and 147\,hPa) also have larger (i.e., poorer) precision values that are sometimes large enough to trigger the ``too much a~priori influence'' negative-precision flag. In such cases, the retrieved value will probably be smaller than the true abundance because the retrieval is being pulled towards the a~priori value of zero ppbv. Nonetheless, greatly enhanced \ch{SO2} is being observed, reflecting the detection of a volcanic plume. Profiles where the precision is set to zero, however, should be omitted from scientific analyses.

  \evenstatusrule
  
  \item[Quality field:] \screeningrule{Only profiles having \texttt{Quality} \emph{greater} than 0.95 should be used.} As with water vapor, this value is where the quality versus yield sharply drops, indicating the transition from well-fit profiles toward more poorly fit ones, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:so2datayields}.
  \item[Convergence field:] \screeningrule{Only profiles having \texttt{Convergence} \emph{less} than 1.03 should be used.}
\end{description}
\end{nofloatpages}

\subsection{Artifacts}

High values of \ch{SO2} accompanied by negative precisions are likely to be underestimated due to the influence of the a~priori, which biases the retrieved abundances toward zero. The atmospheric background \ch{SO2} (\mytexttilde0.1\,ppbv) cannot be measured by MLS, even with extensive data averaging, as systematic errors will induce biases of a few ppbv.

Figure~\ref{fig:so2overlay} compares total column \ch{SO2} measured by OMI and stratospheric column \ch{SO2} calculated by MLS for two days following the Sarychev eruption. It is clear that MLS detects the main plume dispersal features. It also appears that MLS columns are often smaller than those from OMI\@. Interpreting the significance of this difference is not straightforward, given that OMI has to make assumptions regarding the profile shape and can observe \ch{SO2} down to the boundary layer. The MLS column begins at 215\,hPa and is integrated upward, thus neglecting tropospheric contributions. Another limitation is that OMI only makes measurements during the day, whereas MLS measures day and night. Since plumes generally move relatively quickly over the \mbox{12-hour} MLS ascending/descending measurement separation time, MLS nighttime measurements often miss those seen by OMI during the day (12 hours apart) or detect plume features differently.

Figure~\ref{fig:so2timeseries} shows a comparison between \viii, \viv, \prevv, and \thisv for the portion of an MLS orbit with the maximum \ch{SO2} measured on 16~June 2009. As expected, \prevv and \thisv show similar amounts of \ch{SO2} at 215--68\,hPa. The MLS retrieval shows that the \ch{SO2} is concentrated between 147 and 100\,hPa.
