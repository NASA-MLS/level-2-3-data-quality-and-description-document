\subsection{Introduction}

 MLS temperature is retrieved primarily from bands near \ch{O2} spectral lines at 118\,GHz and 239\,GHz that are measured with MLS radiometers \texttt{R1A:118}/\texttt{R1B:118} and \texttt{R3:240}, respectively. The isotopic 239\,GHz line is the primary source of temperature information in the troposphere, with information on temperature in the stratosphere and above deriving from the 118\,GHz \ch{O2} line. As a result of the work described in Section~\ref{sec:V6TemperatureWork}, the MLS \thisv temperature product substantially improves upon the \prevv product in the middle and upper stratosphere and lower mesosphere, with a reduction of vertical oscillations in the stratosphere, an up to 30\% improvement in vertical resolution, and up to 20\% improvement in measurement precision. In the upper troposphere and in the upper mesosphere and above, the \thisv product is very similar to that of \prevv, and at those levels both versions are generally similar to the \viii product and to the \vii product described by~\citet{SchwartzEtal:2008:Validation}. MLS \thisv temperature has an approximately $-1\,$K bias with respect to correlative measurements in the troposphere and lower stratosphere. Table~\ref{tab:TemperatureSummary} summarizes the measurement precision, resolution, and modeled and observed biases. The following subsections provide details.

\subsection{Differences between \thisv and \prevv}
\label{subsec:v6v5T}
The most significant differences between the \thisv and \prevv temperature products result from changes in the way radiances from the 25-channel filterbank centered on the 118.75~GHz \ch{O2} line (Band~1) are used in the retrieval, as described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:V6TemperatureWork}.  The general philosophy of the MLS science team is to avoid adjusting radiances to improve the consistency between MLS products and correlative data. As part of \thisv development, Band-1 channel filter shapes were adjusted, not in order to obtain a ``desired'' retrieved temperature, but rather to make an off-line retrieval of \ch{O2} (developed specifically for the purpose of investigating channel shifts) consistent with the known mixing ratio of \ch{O2} in the stratosphere and lower mesosphere. These changes have the additional benefit of reconciling differences between the ``radiometric'' and ``hydrostatic'' information on temperature obtained from the \mbox{118-GHz} observations (see the following paragraph and Section~\ref{sec:V6TemperatureWork}), allowing radiances from the central portion of Band~1 that had been excluded from recent retrieval versions to be used in \thisv.

Figure\,\ref{fig:RadresidScanresidV6V5}(a) and (c) show, for \prevv and \thisv, respectively, differences between measured and fitted radiances in MLS Band~1.  White dots in panel (a) indicate where radiances were excluded from the \prevv and earlier retrievals. In some cases these daily averaged differences between observed and predicted radiances exceed \pm5\,K. Use of these channels/levels in \thisv (Figure\,\ref{fig:RadresidScanresidV6V5}(c)) provides the retrieval with pressure information along the limb ray paths from the width of the pressure-broadened \ch{O2} line. Saturated radiances from the bottom of the limb scan provide temperature information, much as do radiances from nadir-looking passive microwave temperature profilers. The improved pressure information of \thisv combined with height information from the scan model provide additional temperature information through the assumption of hydrostatic balance, improving the vertical resolution of the \thisv temperature retrieval relative to that of \prevv by 20--30\% in the upper stratosphere and lower mesosphere (10--0.1\,hPa) while also improving the retrieval's estimated precision by 10--20\% at these levels.  

Figure\,\ref{fig:RadresidScanresidV6V5}(b) and (d) show typical ``scan residual'' histograms for \prevv and \thisv, respectively. The scan residual measures the difference between two estimates of limb tangent-point altitude, one derived from instrument and spacecraft pointing information, the other from the temperature and tangent-point pressure retrievals, assuming hydrostatic balance applies in the atmosphere.  The ``optimal'' solution is one that has these two measures agreeing to the degree expected given uncertainties in the quantities involved. Very small \prevv scan residuals in the lower mesosphere (50--65\,km) are consistent with the lack of line-width information provided to the \prevv retrieval at these levels; the tangent-point pressure has a large uncertainty, so the retrieval cleaves to the pointing information. Higher in the mesosphere, the \mbox{Band-22} high-resolution spectrometer centered on the \mbox{118-GHz} \ch{O2} line provides linewidth information in the altitude regions where pressure broadening dominates the line width. At the very top of the scan, Doppler broadening dominates, limiting the ability to measure pressure.

The \thisv temperature retrieval uses Goddard Earth Observing System for Instrument Teams (GEOS-IT, \url{https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/gmao-products/geos-it/}, system release GEOS-5.29.4) as its a~priori in the upper troposphere and stratosphere, replacing the GEOS-5 ``Forward Processing for Instrument Teams'' (FP-IT) used as a~priori in \prevv. For both products, the same climatology is used above (at pressures smaller than) 1\,hPa.  Throughout the rest of this section, ``GEOS-5'' without additional qualification refers to GEOS-IT\@.  Zonal-mean differences between the GEOS versions are as large as 2.5\,K in the upper stratosphere, with scatter as large as 4.5\,K at 2\,hPa, but zonal-mean differences in retrieved temperature due to the changed a~priori are less than 0.5\,K.

Figure~\ref{fig:v6v5_T_Res_Prec} shows some of the major differences between the \thisv and \prevv temperature products.  Panel (a) shows the globally and annually (2021) averaged \thisv and \prevv a~priori and retrieved temperature products, while panel (b) show differences of these profiles from the \thisv a~priori.  The \prevv a~priori is \mytexttilde1\,K warmer in the uppermost stratosphere (1.4--2.2\,hPa) than the \thisv a~priori (green line in panel (b)). This difference results from vertically sharp structure near the stratopause that can be seen to ``leak'' into the retrieved \prevv product (red) of panel (b). A reduction in vertical oscillations in \thisv (blue) in the middle and upper stratosphere and lower mesosphere (\mytexttilde30--0.1\,hPa) is evident, relative to the \prevv profile (red), but \thisv still has a global bias relative to a~priori in the upper stratosphere that approaches $-1$\,K. Figure\,\ref{fig:v6v5_T_Res_Prec}(c) and~(d) show the more than 30\% improvement in \thisv vertical resolution relative to \prevv in the upper stratosphere and lower mesosphere.  Figure~\ref{fig:v6v5_T_Res_Prec}(e) and~(f) similarly show the \mytexttilde20\% improvement in \thisv retrieval precision relative to that of \prevv in the upper stratosphere and lower mesosphere.

The standard \thisv and \prevv temperature products are both taken from the \texttt{CorePlusR3} phase, which uses radiances from the 240\nobreakdash-GHz radiometer (\texttt{R3:240}) in addition to those from the 118\nobreakdash-GHz radiometer (\texttt{R1A:118})/\texttt{R1B:118}). The 235\nobreakdash-GHz \ch{O^{18}O} line in Band~8 provides upper-tropospheric temperature and tangent-point pressure information, supplementing that from the \ch{O2} line at 118\,GHz, the primary source of retrieved temperature and tangent-point pressure in the stratosphere and mesosphere. In addition to temperature, \texttt{CorePlusR3} simultaneously retrieves standard products for a number of atmospheric constituents. The \texttt{CorePlusR3} temperature and GPH, along with the accompanying tangent-point pressures, are used for all subsequent constituent retrievals, so standard temperature and GPH are internally consistent with the suite of constituent retrievals.

MLS is generally insensitive to the presence of thin clouds, but scattering in the cores of convective storms can produce significant perturbations in MLS retrieved quantities, including temperature. Indeed, a~priori knowledge of temperature from meteorological analyses is often so good in the lower and middle atmosphere that perturbations of a few percent (an amount typically considered to be negligible for trace-gas constituent retrievals) are ``significant'' for temperature. In the troposphere, where a~priori knowledge may be good to on the order of 1--2\,K, temperatures from an analysis such as \mbox{GEOS-5} (the retrieval a~priori) may be more reliable than the MLS retrieval product, and differences between the MLS and \mbox{GEOS-5} temperatures can be useful in the identification of retrieval artifacts. Cloud-induced perturbations generally occur in the tropics and midlatitudes (where convective storms occur) in both the \thisv and \prevv retrieval versions. In unscreened data at retrieval levels 316--178\,hPa, clouds produce primarily negative outliers in \thisv and \prevv. The top panels of Figure~\ref{fig:v6v5_215hPa_pcolorhist2} show this behavior at 215\,hPa. Conversely, at 121\,hPa, outliers are primarily positive in \thisv and \prevv (not shown).

Temperature is retrieved, in both \prevv and \thisv, up to the 0.0001\,hPa level, but the 0.00046\,hPa level is typically near the top tangent point of the MLS scan, while the 0.00022\,hPa surface is often above the height of the highest tangent point in the MLS limb scan (depending upon latitude). The estimated precision of the retrieval at both of those levels is less than half of the precision of the retrieval a~priori. However, as can be seen from Figure~\ref{fig:AvkTemperature_HR} for both latitudes shown, the averaging kernel of the 0.00046\,hPa retrieval level is sharply peaked at 0.00046\,hPa, while the averaging kernel of the 0.00022\,hPa retrieval level shows a larger contribution from 0.00046\,hPa than from 0.00022\,hPa. As was the case in \prevv, the precision of the 0.00022\,hPa level in \thisv is set negative ``by hand'' to advise against its casual use, but the 0.00022\,hPa level may have value for some scientific studies.

\subsection{Resolution}
\onestandardavk{Temperature_HR}{Temperature}

The vertical and horizontal resolution of the MLS temperature measurement are defined to be the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the vertically and horizontally collapsed retrieval averaging kernels. Averaging kernels are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:AvkTemperature_HR}, and resolution is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:TemperatureSummary}. Vertical resolution, shown on the left panel, is 3--4\,km at most levels from 261\,hPa to 10\,hPa and degrades to 4--6\,km at 3.16--0.1\,hPa, 11\,km at 0.01\,hPa, and 13\,km at 0.00046\,hPa. Along-track resolution is \mytexttilde170\,km from 261\,hPa to 0.1\,hPa and degrades to 280\,km at 0.001\,hPa and to 316\,km at 0.00046\,hPa. The cross-track resolution is set by the \mbox{6-km} width of the MLS \mbox{240-GHz} field of view in the troposphere and by the \mbox{12-km} width of the MLS 118\,GHz field of view in the stratosphere and above. The longitudinal separation of MLS measurements, which is determined by the Aura orbit, is 10\degsym--20\degsym{} over middle and low latitudes and finer in polar regions.

\subsection{Precision}

The precision of the MLS \thisv temperature measurement is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:TemperatureSummary} and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:v6v5_T_Res_Prec}(e) and~(f). Precision quantifies the random uncertainty in the measurements, impacts of which can be assumed to be reduced when multiple profiles are averaged. The retrieval software returns an estimate of precision based upon the propagation of radiometric noise and a~priori uncertainties through the measurement system. These values, which range from \mytexttilde0.6\,K in the lower stratosphere to \mytexttilde2.5\,K in the mesosphere and \mytexttilde4\,K at 0.00046\,hPa, are given for selected levels. The ``observed scatter'' column gives the root mean square of differences of values from successive orbits (divided by the square root of two, as we are looking at the difference of two noisy signals) for latitudes and seasons where longitudinal variability is small and/or is a function only of local solar time. The smallest values found, which are in the tropics in the troposphere and in high-latitude summer in the stratosphere and mesosphere, are taken to be those least impacted by atmospheric variability and are what is reported in this column. These values are 0.5--0.8\,K larger than those estimated by the measurement system in the troposphere and lower stratosphere and a factor of \mytexttilde1.4 larger from the middle stratosphere through the mesosphere.

\subsection{Accuracy}

Retrieval accuracy is estimated both by modeling the impact of uncertainties in measurement and retrieval parameters that could lead to systematic errors and through comparisons of retrieved values with correlative data sets.  The ``modeled accuracy'' column of Table~\ref{tab:TemperatureSummary} gives estimates from the propagation of parameter uncertainties, as discussed by \citet{SchwartzEtal:2008:Validation}.

\subsection{Data screening}
\label{sec:TemperatureScreening}
\begin{description}
  \item[Pressure range:] \screeningrule{261--0.00046\,hPa} \standardrangestatement \standardprecisionrule \evenstatusrule

  \item[Quality:] \textbf{Only use profiles with \texttt{Quality} greater than 0.2 for the 83\,hPa level and smaller pressures, and profiles with \texttt{Quality} greater than 0.9 at 100\,hPa and larger pressures.} Profiles with \texttt{Quality} less than or equal to 0.9 comprise 1.4\% of all data and 4.8\% of profiles in the tropics.

  \item[Convergence:] \convergencerule{1.03} This \texttt{Convergence} criterion rejects \textless0.1\% of profiles, although chunks with convergence slightly over this target do not contain manifestly pathological profiles. The primary purpose of this criterion is to reject profiles with extremely poor convergence that may be expected to reflect poor retrieval behavior.

  \item[Cloud Screening:] The ``low cloud'' bit of the temperature \texttt{Status} field does not contain useful information in \thisv, so it cannot be used to screen temperature, in contrast with some earlier versions. Cloud impacts can instead largely be removed using the ice water content (IWC) product, rejecting profiles between 261--100\,hPa for which the 215\,hPa value of IWC is greater than 0.005\,g/m\cp{3}. Implementation of this criteria requires loading the \thisv IWC swath but accomplishes most of the rejection of cloud-induced outliers seen in Figure~\ref{fig:v6v5_68-316hPa_linehists}. This criterion removes 4.7\% of UT profiles globally and 15\% of profiles in the tropics (20\degsym{}N--20\degsym{}S).

  \item[Precision:] The \texttt{L2gpPrecision} field can be used to further eliminate outliers that are believed to be the result of thick clouds, primarily in the tropics. If careful screening of the troposphere is required, levels 261--178\,hPa should be avoided if any of the following criteria are met:
    \begin{description}
      \item[At 316\,hPa:] \texttt{L2gpPrecision} \textgreater\,1.1\,K \textbf{and} latitude \textgreater\,\textminus60\degsym\ (i.e., this rule only applies at latitudes north of 60\degsym{}S)
      \item[At 261\,hPa:] \texttt{L2gpPrecision} \textgreater\,0.7\,K
      \item[At 215\,hPa:] \texttt{L2gpPrecision} \textgreater\,0.825\,K
    \end{description}
\end{description}

\subsection{Artifacts}
Persistent vertically oscillating biases in \prevv temperature with respect to analyses and correlative measurements have been greatly reduced in \thisv in the stratosphere but not completely eliminated in the lower stratosphere and upper troposphere. The impact of clouds is generally limited to tropospheric levels and to the tropics and, to a lesser extent, midlatitudes. After recommended screening, negative departures from a~priori of greater than 10\,K in the tropics (believed almost always to be cloud-induced artifacts) are seen in only 0.2\% of tropical profiles. Flagging of clouds is discussed above. Further discussion of artifacts may be found in \citet{SchwartzEtal:2008:Validation}.

\sloppypar\citet{SchwartzEtal:2008:Validation} describe detailed comparisons of MLS \vii temperature with products from the Goddard Earth Observing System, version~5 \citep{RieneckerEtal:2007} (\mbox{GEOS-5}), the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts \citep[e.g.,][]{SimmonsEtal05} (ECMWF), the CHAllenging Minisatellite Payload (CHAMP) \citep{WickertEtal01}, the combined Atmospheric Infrared Sounder / Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit (AIRS/AMSU), the Sounding of the Atmosphere using Broadband Radiometry (SABER) \citep{MlynczakAndRussell:1995}, the Halogen Occultation Experiment \citep{HervigEtal:1996} (HALOE), and the Atmospheric Chemistry Experiment Fourier Transform Spectrometer \citep{BernathEtal:2005} (ACE-FTS), as well as to radiosondes from the global network. Here, we compare \thisv temperature with temperature from \mbox{GEOS-5} and the Constellation Observing System for Meteorology, Ionsophere and Climate (\mbox{COSMIC-1}) \citep{AnthesEtal:2008} in the upper troposphere to the upper stratosphere and with SABER and ACE in the upper stratosphere, mesosphere, and  lower thermosphere. Figure~\ref{fig:TempSummary2} shows the global-mean biases in the left panel and the $1\sigma$ scatter about the mean in the right panel for these comparisons.  \citet{SchwartzEtal:2008:Validation} showed agreement to generally within \mytexttilde1\,K between assimilation (ECMWF and \mbox{GEOS-5}) and AIRS, radiosondes, and radio-occultation instruments such as \mbox{COSMIC-1} and CHAMP in the upper troposphere and stratosphere, so all of these correlative data sets are not included here.   From 261\,hPa to 68\,hPa, MLS \thisv temperature has a $-$2.5\,K to 0\,K vertically oscillating bias with respect to \mbox{GEOS-5} and \mbox{COSMIC-1}, settling to a $-$0.5\,K bias in the upper stratosphere. Between 1\,hPa and 0.001\,hPa, MLS has generally consistent vertically oscillating biases with respect to SABER and ACE-FTS, with MLS-minus-SABER biases of $+1$\,K~to~$-5$\,K between 1\,hPa and 0.01\,hPa, increasing in magnitude to $-10$\,K at 0.001\,hPa. MLS-minus-ACE biases are \mytexttilde1\,K more negative in the mesosphere and \mytexttilde2\,K less negative in the lower thermosphere than MLS-minus-SABER biases.
